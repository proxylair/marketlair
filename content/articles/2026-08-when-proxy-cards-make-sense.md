Proxy cards are one of the more misunderstood parts of the hobby, and the
"should I use one?" question comes up constantly.

**What a proxy actually is.** A proxy is a stand-in for a real card. At the
simplest end, that might be a card name written on a piece of paper for
testing a deck. At the other end are professionally printed custom cards
with alternate artwork, premium finishes, and the full rules text of the
card they're representing.

The purpose is basically the same: letting you play, test, or enjoy a card
without putting the genuine version on the table.

**Where they're allowed -- and where they're not.** This is the important
distinction.

Sanctioned tournament play generally requires genuine, tournament-legal
cards. A custom proxy isn't a workaround for entering an official event
with a card you don't own. Tournament officials may sometimes issue
substitute cards under specific circumstances, but that's different from
bringing your own proxy.

Casual play is another story.

Commander groups, kitchen-table players, testing sessions, cubes, and
other casual environments often allow proxies depending on the people
involved. Some local game stores allow them during casual play or
particular unsanctioned events as well.

The simplest rule is also the best one: ask the people you're playing
with.

If you're playing an organized event, check with the store or tournament
organizer. If you're sitting down for Commander with friends, have the
conversation before the game starts.

**Where proxies start making a lot of sense.** Price is the obvious
example.

At those prices, most players aren't deciding whether to buy four copies.
They're deciding whether they'll ever play with the card at all.

But proxies don't only make sense when a card costs thousands of dollars.

A $60 card you aren't sure belongs in your deck is still $60. A $100
staple you want to use across four casual Commander decks is still $400
if you buy four copies. An expensive collectible you already own may also
be something you'd rather keep safely in a binder than shuffle
repeatedly.

That's where proxies can be genuinely useful:

- **Testing before buying.** Play the deck first and find out whether the
  expensive card is actually worth purchasing.
- **Casual deck building.** Build around cards that would otherwise put
  the deck outside your budget.
- **Protecting valuable originals.** Keep the collectible copy protected
  while using a stand-in during casual games, when your playgroup allows
  it.
- **Multiple decks.** Avoid buying the same expensive casual staple over
  and over just because several decks use it.
- **Custom artwork.** Turn a card you already enjoy into something
  personal to your deck, character, theme, or playgroup.

That doesn't make the original card worthless. In fact, collecting and
playing don't have to be the same thing.

You can want the genuine card for your collection while also deciding
that shuffling a four-figure collectible into a casual deck doesn't make
much sense.

**When a proxy doesn't make sense.** If you're preparing for sanctioned
competitive play, build and practice with the requirements of that event
in mind. You don't want to discover at registration that cards you've
been relying on aren't legal for the tournament.

They're also a bad fit when the people you're playing with aren't
comfortable with them. Casual games work because everyone agrees on what
kind of game they're sitting down to play.

And if you're buying something represented as a genuine collectible,
that's an entirely different issue. A custom proxy should be treated as a
custom proxy -- not passed off or sold as an authentic card.
